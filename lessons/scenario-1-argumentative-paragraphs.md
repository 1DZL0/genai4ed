## 3. What the teacher explains (phase 1)

1. **One argument per paragraph**, following this structure:
   1. **Argument:** the first sentence presents an argument that directly addresses the essay question.
   2. **Justification:** 2 to 3 reasons that support the argument.
   3. **Examples:** relevant examples from social, political or everyday life, and/or scientific evidence.
2. **Develop the justification.** Focus on positive or negative outcomes for the person or society, such as
   effects on knowledge, skills, quality of life or the economy. Reasons must be developed, not just stated.
3. **Connectives** ("for example", "therefore", "for this reason") are used appropriately, but not too often.
4. **Accurate language:** complete sentences, correct grammar and syntax, vocabulary suited to a formal essay
   (no slang), words used accurately.

## 4. Worked example (phase 2)

### Retrieval: what the class recalls from the text and video

| Criteria for choosing a profession | Possible consequences |
|---|---|
| Future career prospects | Job security, chances of promotion |
| Salary | Financial security, quality of life |
| Personal talents | Satisfaction, doing the job well |
| Lifestyle | Free time, stress, family life |

| Influences on career choice | Examples |
|---|---|
| Positive | Access to information, education, family support |
| Negative | Family pressure, peer influence |

### Model paragraph on the board **[to confirm: draft wording]**

> Money is an important criterion when choosing a profession. Firstly, a good salary gives people financial
> security, because they can pay for a home, food and their children's education without constant worry.
> Secondly, a higher income improves quality of life, since it allows people to travel, take up hobbies and
> rest properly. For example, engineers and doctors often earn enough to support a family comfortably and
> still save for the future. For this reason, many young people consider salary carefully before they decide
> on a career.

The teacher points out on the board: argument (sentence 1), reason 1 developed with an outcome, reason 2
developed with an outcome, a real example, and a closing connective.

## 5. Tasks (phase 3)

Every student does the same two tasks.

| # | Type | Task |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Written response | **Write one paragraph** that presents and supports **one criterion for choosing a profession**. |
| 2 | Written response | **Write one paragraph** that presents and explains **one factor that may negatively influence a person's career choice**. |

Students may type and submit a paragraph at a time, or part of a paragraph. They decide when each paragraph is
final and submit it.

---

## 6. How much the tutor may help

The tutor's aim is to **scaffold the writing, never to write the paragraph for the student**. Help moves up
one step only after the student has tried, and fades as the student starts to succeed.

| Step | What the tutor may do | Example wording |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Have a go | Ask the student to write first. | "Write your argument in one sentence, even if it's rough. I'll help from there." |
| 2. Think back | An active retrieval prompt about the studied material. | "Think back to the video. What factors were mentioned that may influence a person's choice of profession?" |
| 3. A cue | Point to the part of the structure that is missing. | "You have an argument and one reason. What does the structure on the board say comes next?" |
| 4. A hint | Narrow the possibilities without giving the answer. | "Think about what a good salary allows someone to do for their family." |
| 5. An example idea | One relevant idea or example from the studied material. | "In the video, one person chose engineering for its future career prospects." |
| 6. Model sentences | One or two model sentences to build on. | "For example, a doctor's salary can support a family and pay for their children's education." |
| 7. The whole paragraph | **Not allowed in this lesson.** | |

**Limit for this lesson:** up to step 6, model sentences.

### Language help (any time during writing)

A student may ask, for example, "How can I say this more formally?" or "Is this sentence grammatically
correct?" The tutor may:

- suggest more accurate or more formal words and phrases;
- explain the grammar, syntax or vocabulary problem;
- give an example of how a word or structure is used correctly;
- **rewrite a single sentence only after failed attempts or when the student says they are struggling**, and
  always explain what it changed and why.

### Feedback when the student asks

For example "What do you think of my paragraph?" or "What would you suggest?". The tutor comments on:

- **Content:** is the justification relevant and fully developed, with supporting examples?
- **Language:** vocabulary, grammar, syntax, register.
- **Structure:** organisation, placement of ideas and examples, use of connectives.

Students may revise their work after feedback.

## 8. Rubric

**[to confirm: points]** The scenario names the three areas; the points are a draft.

| Area | Points | Full marks looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Content | 4 | A clear argument that answers the question; 2 to 3 reasons, each developed with an outcome; relevant real examples or evidence. |
| Language | 3 | Complete, grammatically correct sentences; accurate vocabulary suited to a formal essay; no slang. |
| Structure | 3 | Ideas in a logical order; examples placed after the reasons they support; connectives used appropriately and not too often. |
| **Total** | **10** | |

### Scoring and feedback on submission

1. The AI scores each submitted paragraph against this rubric, with a reason for each area, and names the
   specific areas to develop in content, language and structure.
2. **The score is a suggestion.** The teacher confirms or changes it before the student sees it.
3. The student then sees the score, what went well, and what to work on next.
